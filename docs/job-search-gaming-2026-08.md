## 3. The five job titles are not interchangeable

This is the part most people get wrong, and it decides which applications are worth writing. The
role definitions below are drawn from the actual postings I read today, not from general knowledge.

**1. UI Designer (games).** Owns the look and layout of menus, HUD, store, progression. Heavy visual
craft, works inside someone else's art direction, usually delivers production-ready assets. Riot's
and Supercell's postings both sit here. **Portfolio it wants:** screens, systems, style guides,
motion. **You are close** — this is the nearest thing to what your site already demonstrates — but
these postings expect engine familiarity and raster asset craft.

**2. UX Designer (games).** Flows, wireframes, information architecture, usability, player
comprehension. EA calls this "Experience Designer". **Portfolio it wants:** flows, problem framing,
before/after, research. **You have this**, and it is the strongest overlap with your existing case
studies — Bearings and the UC Davis Rec redesign are both exactly this shape.

**3. UI Artist.** A **different discipline**. 9-slice frames, icon sets, texture atlases, UI VFX,
material and lighting work on interface elements. Epic's two UI Artist roles and Supercell's Senior
UI Artist sit here. **Portfolio it wants:** art. **Do not apply to these.** Your work is
typographic, vector and CSS-driven; you will be compared against people with a raster art practice
and you will lose, and it will teach you the wrong lesson about your chances.

**4. Technical UI Designer.** Builds the UI inside the engine — Unreal UMG, Unity UI Toolkit —
wiring data, states and animation. Epic's *Senior Technical UI Designer* and Kabam's *UI Technical
Designer* sit here. **This is arguably your best long-term match in all of games**, because you
genuinely ship code and most designers do not. But **every posting I found is Senior or contract.**
There is no junior rung today. Treat it as a two-to-four-year target that your existing habits are
already building toward. (a on the postings, c on the fit)

**5. Product Designer on a games platform.** Discord, Roblox, Twitch, Rockstar's web/marketing
product team, Digital Extremes' web team, Zynga's Experience Designers. **This is a product design
job that happens to be at a game company.** Same portfolio you already have. Better pay. Tech-company
hiring norms.

**Which of these you are competitive for today, honestly:**

- **Today, no changes:** #5 (product designer on a games platform), and #2 at the mobile tier.
- **After roughly one month of focused work:** #1 and #2 at the mobile/free-to-play tier — needs one
  Unity artefact and one controller-navigation artefact. See Section 4.
- **Not today, and by design not for a while:** #4 — no junior rung exists.
- **Never, on this portfolio:** #3. Different craft.

## 4. The gap, stated plainly

A games UI portfolio contains things yours does not. Some of those gaps are real blockers. Some are
vocabulary problems you can close in a weekend by renaming work you have already shipped. Here is
which is which.

### The real blockers — these need actual work

**1. Engine implementation. This is the big one.**
Jam City's junior-tier posting requires *"Experience integrating UI assets into Unity."* (a) Epic's
UI work is staffed with programmers. Kabam's is a "UI Technical Designer". Games UI lives inside
Unity's UI Toolkit/UGUI or Unreal's UMG, and a designer who cannot open the engine hands off to
someone who can — which is why so many of these titles have "Technical" or "Programmer" in them.

**Everything you have shipped is DOM, Canvas and hand-written JavaScript.** That is not nothing — it
is more real-time implementation than 95% of junior design applicants have — but it is not the
engine they use.

**The fix, and it is the single highest-leverage thing on this entire list:** rebuild **one** screen
you have already designed in Unity UI Toolkit. The **tournament bracket** is the right screen — it
has hierarchy, state, data binding, and a clear before/after. Two weekends. Then your line stops
being "I have never used Unity" and becomes "here is the same screen I built in the browser, rebuilt
in Unity, and here is what changed about the design when the layout system changed." That second
sentence is a better answer than most people with Unity on their CV can give. (c, but grounded in
what the postings ask for)

**2. Controller-first navigation and focus states.**
Every interface you have built is pointer- or touch-driven. Console and handheld UI is driven by a
D-pad and sticks, which means the designer owns: which element has focus on entry, how focus travels
between elements, what happens at the edges (wrap or stop), how focus is *visibly* communicated, and
the button-prompt legend at the bottom of the screen that changes glyphs per platform.

This is a design problem, not an engine problem, and it is the fastest genuine skill you can add.
**Your tournament bracket is an unusually good test case** — a bracket is a two-dimensional graph
where "the next match" is not "the box to the right", so focus traversal in a bracket is a real
design question with a defensible answer. Add D-pad navigation to it and you have a portfolio piece
almost nobody at your level has.

**3. UI art craft.** Covered above. Not closable, and you should not try. Aim at UI *Designer* and
UX Designer postings, never UI *Artist*.

**4. Console certification and platform requirements.** Title requirement checklists (Sony's TRC,
Microsoft's XR, Nintendo's guidelines), safe zones for TV overscan, mandatory system messaging,
licensed button glyphs, controller disconnect handling. You have zero exposure and you cannot claim
any. **This is knowledge, not portfolio** — you can read about it and speak sensibly in an interview
within a day, but do not put it on a CV. It only matters for console roles, and console roles have
no junior tier anyway, so it is the lowest-priority gap on this list. (c)

**5. Localisation length variance.** Games ship in 12–20 languages simultaneously and German and
Russian strings run 30–40% longer than English. Games UI designers design layouts that survive that.
Your site is English-only with tight, deliberate typography — which is the *opposite* instinct. This
one is close to free to fix: add a longest-string test to one screen and show it. (c)

### The weekend reframes — work you have already done, described in the right vocabulary

You are sitting on more relevant material than you think. It is described in portfolio language when
it needs to be described in games language. Artefact by artefact:

**The soccer match** (`play-engine.js`, ~3,976 lines) → **a HUD case study.**
This is real-time state with spectator legibility under chaos, which is precisely what a games UI
interview probes. Reframe it around the question: *what must a viewer read in 200 milliseconds while
twelve heads and a ball are moving?* Score, possession, who has the ball, ability cooldowns. Then
name the split you have **already made without naming it**: the heads and the ball are **diegetic**
(they exist in the world), the score and the menu are **non-diegetic** (overlaid chrome). You made
that call. You just have not used the words. Add the words and it becomes a HUD case study.

**The tournament** (`play-tournament.js`, ~2,008 lines) → **flow, spectator UX and pacing.**
Single-elimination state machine, seeding, a bracket, broadcast-style match previews. Two specific
things to pull out:
- **The bracket is a "where am I / what happens next" problem**, which is the core UX question of
  every tournament, ladder and progression screen ever shipped.
- **The celebration delay.** You deliberately hold the celebration before advancing. That is
  *pacing*, and pacing is game feel, and game feel is the thing UI designers get asked about that
  product designers never are. Write down why the delay exists and what it would feel like at half
  and double the length. That paragraph is worth more than another screen.
- Bonus: supporting both 8 and 12 entrants is a **responsive information-density** problem, which is
  the same muscle as designing for TV distance.

**The marble race qualifier** (12 racers) → **legibility under motion.**
How do you keep twelve near-identical objects distinguishable while they move fast? That is the same
design problem as a minimap, a killfeed, a team-colour system, or a scoreboard in a battle royale.
The position-based anti-stuck logic is systems design. Frame it as "identity at speed" and it stops
looking like a toy.

**Head Maker** (`headmaker.html`, ~1,640 lines) → **an avatar/character customisation system.**
A tool that cuts a photo of a face into an animated character. Every live-service game has an avatar
or customisation system and **it is almost always owned by UI**. Frame it as a pipeline with design
decisions: what the input constraints are, what happens on a bad photo, what you show the player
while it processes, and what the failure state looks like. Failure states are the part junior
portfolios always skip and interviewers always ask about.

**The Play menu, mood picker and current-players grid** → **front-end / shell design.**
Menus, settings, lobbies, stores, progression. Here is the thing most people entering games UI do
not know: **the majority of games UI work is the shell, not the HUD.** You already have shell work.
Say so.

**The site's 8fps stop-motion motion system** → **game feel and juice.**
A deliberate, consistent motion language with a stated rule, applied across a whole product. In games
vocabulary that is a *feel* system. It is also unusual and memorable, which matters more in a games
portfolio review than in a product design one.

### What you genuinely do not have, and should not pretend to

Shipped commercial game credits. Localisation testing at scale. Games accessibility beyond web WCAG
(colourblind modes, subtitle systems, remappable controls, difficulty accommodations). Working inside
an art direction you did not set. Live-ops economy and monetisation UI. Console platform experience.

None of these are fatal for a first job. All of them are things a hiring manager will discover in
five minutes, so name them yourself first.

## 5. The one thing this whole document is really telling you

The two live junior roles I found are: a mobile games UI seat at **$75,000–$110,000** in San
Francisco, and a junior product design seat at **CAD $50,000–$60,000** in Ontario.

The gaming-adjacent tier, same cities, same week: Discord's Product Designer, Growth at
**$148,000–$166,500** base plus equity. (a, all three)

That is not a small difference and it is not an artefact of cherry-picking — it is the two ends of
the same sweep, on the same day.

**One important refinement, so you do not carry away a lazier version of this.** "Games pays less"
is not true across the board — Section 6a shows Riot, Epic, Roblox, Discord and Twitch all sitting at
or above the Bay Area product design median on total compensation, while PlayStation, EA and Blizzard
sit well below it. The consistent pattern is not games-versus-tech; it is **platform companies versus
AAA studios**, and the mechanism is **equity**: the studio roles frequently carry **$0 stock**.
Which means the gap is smallest at the platform end of games — which is exactly the tier this section
is pointing you at anyway.

**The hybrid path is the answer to the question you actually asked.** You said you want to get into
gaming UI. You also said you want *"an amazing first job experience"* and that you prefer *"bigger
companies that have a name."* Those three wants point at the same place, and it is not a game studio:

- **Discord** — B2C, social, real-time, gaming-native audience, SF, and a live posting whose stated
  concession is craft. This is already Tier 2 in your main document. In a gaming context it is the
  strongest thing on your list.
- **Roblox** — San Mateo, 219 open roles, a real early-career pipeline that is already posting 2027
  reqs. Design is not in it *today*. That could change during cohort season.
- **Twitch** — SF, but currently one senior design req. Thin.
- **Games companies' product teams** — Rockstar's Visual Product Designer sits in Digital Marketing.
  Digital Extremes' junior role is web and companion app. Zynga's title for UX is "Experience
  Designer". These are product design jobs inside games companies, and they are the bridge.

**And the direction of travel matters.** Moving from a mainstream product design job into games later
is materially easier than the reverse: games UI portfolios read as domain-specific, product design
portfolios read as transferable, and games companies actively hire product designers for their
platform, live-service, storefront and companion-app surfaces. Going games-first narrows your options;
going product-first keeps games open. (c — this is my inference, but it follows directly from the
role definitions in Section 3 and from which titles the studios above are actually posting.)

**None of which means don't apply to Jam City.** It costs one submission, it is a real job in your
city with no experience gate, and the interview alone would teach you more about games UI than a
month of reading. Apply. Just do not restructure your search around it.

### 6b. Volatility — and it has not stopped

I went looking for evidence that the games layoff wave of 2023–2025 was over. **It is not. 2026 is
running worse than 2025, and the industry's own optimistic forecast was falsified within seven
months.**

**First, a caveat that matters.** The two most-cited layoff trackers **disagree by roughly two
times**, so any number needs its source attached:

| Year | **ASGC tracker** (Amir Satvat, Tencent Games) | **videogamelayoffs.com** (Farhan Noor) |
|---|---|---|
| 2023 | 10,500 | over 10,000 |
| 2024 | **15,631** | ~14,600 |
| 2025 | **9,197** | **5,300** |
| 2026 to date | **9,781** (through 26 Jul) | **~4,600** (through ~1 Jul) |
| 2026 forecast | **14,259** | — |

(b — GamesBeat for ASGC; Kotaku, 22 July 2026, quoting Noor. The likely cause of the gap, per Game
Developer, is that public trackers miss quiet reductions, regional cuts, and games teams buried inside
larger corporate restructures.)

**The trend is the same on either tracker: 2026 is ahead of 2025.** On ASGC's numbers, 2026 has
already passed the whole of 2025 with two quarters left, across **105 separate layoff events** —
roughly **0.7 layoff events per working day.** Satvat had projected ~7,500 for 2026 in January,
calling 2025 *"a significant improvement… the industry is getting closer to its equilibrium."* By late
July he had revised to 14,259, **up 78%**, saying *"The pace of layoffs is accelerating faster than our
models initially predicted."* (b)

**And it is concentrated exactly where you live.** North America accounts for **66% of layoff events
and ~79% of affected workers**; the US alone is ~48% of the 2026 total. (b)

### Does anything cut the other way?

Yes, and it deserves a fair hearing — but read the whole of it, because the good news has a sting.

Satvat's own hiring data (b, GamesBeat, 20 April 2026): from 2022 to Q1 2026 the industry **hired
~52,500 while eliminating ~48,000**. Global headcount actually **grew from 750,000 to 754,000
(+0.6%)**, with 9,000–14,000 roles open at any time. His line: *"The industry did not collapse. It
redistributed."*

Now the sting, from the same dataset:

- **North American headcount fell 11.5%** over those four years while global rose 0.6%. The growth
  went to Latin America, Eastern Europe and Asia.
- **North America has ~127,000 job seekers against ~12,000 hires — an 11:1 ratio** (5:1 globally).
- Net new roles are running at **~1,100 a year**, against **30,000–50,000 a year in 2017–2022**.
- Hiring is concentrated at **5–15 years of experience.**

And the sentence that matters most for you, from Satvat's own December 2025 predictions piece, in the
same breath as *"The recovery trend is real and accelerating"*:

> **"Early career pressure will remain extremely high. I do not see entry challenges easing yet."**

(b — https://gamesbeat.com/job-focused-game-industry-predictions-for-2026-amir-satvat/)

**A 2027 headwind worth knowing about:** PwC's *Global Entertainment & Media Outlook 2026–2030*
forecasts the **US games market dipping into negative growth in 2027**, because 2027 is the last year
before the next console generation launches in 2028 — even with *GTA VI* arriving 19 November 2026.
(b — GamesBeat, 21 June 2026)

**What my own board data adds.** The industry is still hiring; it has stopped hiring at the bottom.
Ubisoft has 271 live postings, Sony 200, Roblox 219, Riot 164, 2K 112. Those are not the numbers of an
industry that has stopped. Across all of them, junior design roles: **zero.** (a on the counts, c on
the reading)

**For contrast, the mainstream design market is in better shape** — not good, but better. Nielsen
Norman Group's *State of UX 2026* (16 January 2026) says *"from late 2024 through 2025, the job market
began to stabilize"*, while warning that *"senior practitioners and generalist roles are recovering
faster than entry-level positions"* and that in 2026 *"the supply of aspiring UX professionals will
still outpace open roles, especially at the junior level."* (b —
https://www.nngroup.com/articles/state-of-ux-2026/)

That is the honest comparison: **entry-level is hard everywhere. It is materially harder in games.**

### 6c. The straight read on the trade

You said you want *"an amazing first job experience."* Put the four findings next to each other:

1. **At the entry end, games UI pays roughly half** what the gaming-adjacent tier pays for comparable
   work in the same city — and within games, **UX is the second-cheapest design specialism of six.**
   (a on the bands, b on the survey)
2. **One in three US games workers was laid off in the last two years**; 48% of them still had not
   found work; **game designers were the most-affected profession at 20% of all layoffs**; 2026 is
   running ahead of 2025 and North America is taking two-thirds of it. (a/b)
3. **North America has roughly 127,000 games job seekers against 12,000 hires**, and the industry's
   own most optimistic analyst says *"Early career pressure will remain extremely high."* (b)
4. **There are two junior games UI roles in the entire sweep**, one of which is in Canada. (a)

A first job is worth taking for the team, the mentorship and the shipped work — and games studios can
absolutely give you all three. But **a first job is also worth taking for stability**, because being
laid off at nine months with no shipped credit is genuinely hard to recover from, and roughly half of
the people this industry laid off had not found another games job when GDC asked them.

That is the trade. It is not "games bad, tech good". It is: **the thing you want to design is
available in the adjacent tier at twice the pay with a fraction of the risk, and you can build the
games credibility yourself — you already are.** Section 5 is the recommendation. This section is the
reason for it.

## What I could not reach, and what that does not prove

Being straight about coverage, because absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

- **Xbox proper.** Microsoft's careers API (`gcsservices.careers.microsoft.com`) returned no
  connection at all from my environment across three attempts, and `jobs.careers.microsoft.com`
  redirects to a fully JavaScript-rendered page that returns no listings to a fetcher. **I did not
  enumerate Xbox's design roles.** Blizzard and King I did reach, through the shared Workday tenant.
  **Check Xbox yourself in a browser** — it is the single biggest hole in this document.
- **Niantic's board.** Runs on Phenom; `/careers/jobs` returns 404 and the widget endpoint redirects.
  Given the games business is now Scopely's, this matters much less than it would have a year ago.
- **Unity's board** could only be enumerated as a title list without locations. The titles are
  unambiguous — one UX title in the company, at manager level — but I could not read requirement text.
- **EA, Netflix, Blizzard and King** were reached by keyword search, not full enumeration. A design
  role with an unusual title could have escaped those four searches. Everything else in Section 2 was
  a full board read.
- **Hosts that blocked automated fetching outright** during the research, across both passes:
  `epicgames.com` (all careers URLs, including Epic's own layoffs announcement), `glassdoor.com`
  (every page), `ziprecruiter.com`, `cnbc.com`, `variety.com` (paywall), `ea.gr8people.com`,
  `gamejobs.co`, `careers.roblox.com` (three 60-second timeouts), `layoffs.asgc.gg` (JavaScript
  dashboard), `bls.gov`, and Blizzard's careers search. **These are access failures, not evidence of
  absence** — several of them almost certainly have postings I did not see. Open them in a browser.
- **Two numbers I could not adjudicate.** The two layoff trackers differ by roughly 2× for 2025–26.
  And Microsoft's *company-wide* July 2026 total is reported as ~4,800 by some outlets and ~6,400 by
  others; only the **3,200 Xbox** figure is agreed across sources, so that is the only one I used.
- **One claim circulating that appears to be wrong:** Wikipedia lists Bungie's *Marathon* among 2026
  cancellations. Sony's own June statement says the opposite — that *Marathon* remains part of the
  portfolio. Do not repeat the cancellation claim.
- **Swept after the main pass, nothing found:** **Skillz** — 13 open roles, **zero design**, and note
  the company has moved to Las Vegas and Bengaluru; there is no longer an SF design team to apply to.
  (a)
- **Not swept at all:** NetEase and Tencent's US studios, Behaviour, CD Projekt, Larian, Sega of
  America, Square Enix America, Capcom USA, Playtika, Double Fine, Telltale, WB Games, Bethesda /
  ZeniMax, and the long tail of small Bay Area studios. I could not find working public endpoints for
  these in the time available. Their absence from this document is not a finding.

## What this document is not

- **Not a recommendation to go into games.** Section 5 is deliberately the opposite. You asked
  whether there are companies you can apply to; the honest answer is *two, one of which is in
  Canada*, and the more useful answer is that the gaming-adjacent tier gives you the craft you want
  at roughly double the pay with materially less volatility.

  one would have cost you an afternoon and taught you nothing.

  every link before you write a word of a cover letter.
