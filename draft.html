A transparent scoring model, not a trained one — how it picks, and every source behind it

What the model is, plainly. Nothing here learned anything from data: a player's score is his projected points above the replacement at his position, weighted 1.14× if he is a back, plus a nudge for a quota you have not filled, minus a charge for the distance down to his floor before round ten and plus a payment for the distance up to his ceiling from round ten on, plus a small prior from historical hit rates, minus penalties for an injury flag and for a man who will still be there next round. Every number that moves a pick is printed on that pick's row, which is the point — you can see why it said what it said and overrule it at the table.

Early you pay for the floor, late you pay for the ceiling. Both halves use the same coefficient, 0.55, so the rule cannot disagree with itself. Until this pass only the ceiling half existed: the floor was printed on every row and never scored, which meant that through nine of the fifteen rounds two men with the same projection ranked identically however far apart their bad weeks were. Where a projection sits high inside its own band, the model now says so in the only currency it has.

No quarterback before round eight. Not a preference — a margin. On this year's projections the best quarterback in football is worth +4.8 pts/gm over the one still sitting on the board in round thirteen. The best running back is worth +13.4 over his. Last season Drake Maye finished QB2 at 21.1 pts/gm and Trevor Lawrence QB5 at 19.9, both from well outside the early rounds.

Running back at every slot, not just the good ones. Backs decay roughly twice as fast as receivers: RB13–RB24 average 13.6 pts/gm and RB25–RB36 average 10.5, a 3.1-point step, where the same move at receiver costs 1.8. The model weights running back 1.20× and holds a hard floor — two backs by round four, three by six, four by eight — at all twelve slots.

Almost nothing here is a reach, and the exceptions say so. Every pick is checked against Sleeper's own 12-team PPR ADP. Where Sleeper and Fantasy Football Calculator disagree by more than a round the row says so, because knowing a player goes fifteen picks earlier on the platform you are sitting in front of is the whole edge. In rounds one to six, 212 of the 216 recommendations are inside half a round of their ADP. The four that are not are all receivers, all exactly seven picks ahead, and all the same situation: filling a starting slot the quota demanded left fewer than three men inside the window, and going without a starter costs more than seven picks does. Each of the four prints the number on its row. Later, where a reach costs nothing, the number of picks you are jumping is printed on the row as well.

Where this comes from

ADP: Sleeper 12-team PPR redraft, read from Draft Sharks on 26 August 2026 — the platform and format you actually draft in. Fantasy Football Calculator (7,830 mocks, 19–26 Aug) and Underdog best ball are carried as second opinions; open any row to see all three. Projections, ceilings and floors: WalterPicks 2026 redraft PPR, 26 August 2026.

Historical base rates. Hit rates by round are FFPC drafts 2016–2024, nine seasons (Fantasy Trading Room); bust rates are PPR 2013–2014, a short two-season sample (PlayerProfiler); the quarterback bands are 2009–2011 (PFF); the rounds 3–6 running-back dead zone is 2015–2020 best ball (via PlayerProfiler). They are printed on the row, including where they argue against the pick.

Team context. Weeks 15–17 opponents are the real 2026 schedule from NFL.com, cross-checked game-for-game against FantasyPros. Playoff strength of schedule is Sharp Football, 23 August. Offensive lines are PFF and Sharp, kept separate rather than averaged because where they disagree they disagree hard.

2025 opportunity. Targets, target share, snap share and red-zone work for 113 of the players here, read from FantasyPros' 2025 tables (weeks 1–18; the red-zone rushing report is weeks 1–17 by their own label). Usage predicts next season better than last season's points do — but it is shown, not scored: the projection already has last year's usage inside it, and feeding these numbers back into the model would count the same evidence twice.

What is missing, and why. No routes run or yards per route run — that data sits behind PFF+, Pro Football Reference is behind a bot check, and the ten public figures that do exist mix in the postseason, so they are not here. No receiver or tight-end playoff strength of schedule — Sharp publishes passing and rushing only and no other readable 32-team table exists, so those positions carry nothing rather than a guess. No opportunity data for rookies, which is correct: they have no NFL usage to report. The “same backfield” line is not a depth chart — ordering backs by projection produced one that contradicted the beat reporting, so it claims only who shares a backfield.

On the notes. A note marked report or stat is something written on the page it links to; open the link and read it. A note marked projection only is derived from the WalterPicks numbers and nothing else — there is no report behind it and it is not pretending there is. 408 of the 540 recommendations on the board carry a report or a statistic; the other 132 say plainly that they do not. Camp news moves fast: check the injury flags against your own beat writers on draft day.
